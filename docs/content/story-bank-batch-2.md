## 1. the summer frankenstein was born
**fields:** literature + science + history
**scene:** june 1816, a rented villa by lake geneva. it will not stop raining, and lord byron proposes a contest: everyone writes a ghost story.
**agreed:** byron rented the villa (villa diodati); polidori, the shelleys and claire clairmont were there; the group stayed in during bad weather and set the ghost-story challenge after reading *fantasmagoriana*; mary shelley (18) began what became *frankenstein* (published 1818); polidori wrote *the vampyre*; mount tambora erupted in april 1815 and the following summers were cold.
**contested / say it around:** how much the climate dropped (sources give different figures); how directly the weather "caused" the books. use "some say the gloom helped".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Diodati | https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/villa-diodati | https://artuk.org/discover/stories/sparking-the-gothic-imagination-mary-shelleys-frankenstein-and-polidoris-the-vampyre
**visual:** 1816 portraits and the villa (public domain on wikimedia commons, confirm each file).
**notification:** a volcano on the other side of the world is why frankenstein exists.
**takeaway:** bad weather is sometimes a writing prompt.

## 2. hedy lamarr's secret signal
**fields:** cinema + music + engineering
**scene:** a film star and a composer sketch a way to stop radio-guided torpedoes being jammed.
**agreed:** us patent 2,292,387 "secret communication system" granted 11 august 1942 to hedy kiesler markey (lamarr) and george antheil; a transmitter and receiver hop together across 88 frequencies, set by a paper roll; navy researchers judged the mechanism too bulky; the patent expired in 1959.
**contested:** smithsonian says sylvania engineers later built electronic versions and systems used in the 1962 cuba blockade trace back to that work; do not say the navy "used her patent". say she did not invent wi-fi; frequency hopping is an ancestor of wi-fi, bluetooth and gps. the dinner-party meeting and the "war bonds kisses" details are unverified: leave out or label "some say".
**sources:** https://patents.google.com/patent/US2292387A/en | https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/team-hollywoods-secret-weapons-system-103619955/ | https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/hedy-lamarr
**visual:** the patent drawings (us government document, confirm public-domain status).
**notification:** the most beautiful woman in hollywood filed a patent about piano rolls.
**takeaway:** the idea that outlasts the use is still the idea.

## 3. bouba and kiki
**fields:** psychology + language
**scene:** show two shapes, one spiky, one blobby. which is "bouba" and which is "kiki"?
**agreed:** wolfgang köhler ran the original version in 1929 on tenerife (words "takete" and "baluba", later "maluma"); ramachandran and hubbard (2001) tested english and tamil speakers and found 95 to 98% matched the round shape to "bouba"; a 2022 study of 917 speakers of 25 languages and 10 writing systems found the pattern held, with differing strength.
**contested:** why it happens; some argue spelling shapes the result, though studies with children too young to read cut against that. write "no one is sure why".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouba/kiki_effect | the 2022 study "the bouba/kiki effect is robust across cultures and writing systems"
**visual:** draw our own two shapes (no licence issue).
**notification:** which of these two shapes is called "bouba"? you already know.
**takeaway:** words are not as random as we assume.

## 4. the theft that made the mona lisa famous
**fields:** art + history
**scene:** monday 21 august 1911, the louvre. the painting is gone and nobody notices for about a day.
**agreed:** taken by vincenzo peruggia, a former louvre glazier, hidden under a smock; kept for about two years; he tried to sell it to a florence dealer (alfredo geri), who called in the uffizi director; arrested in florence in late 1913; served a short sentence.
**contested:** exact recovery and return dates (nov or dec 1913, return 1913 or jan 1914); "the theft made her famous" is a popular claim, so write "some say it was the headline that made her a celebrity".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Peruggia | https://www.robertedsel.com/post/2013/12/10/100th-anniversary-of-the-recovery-of-the-mona-lisa
**visual:** the painting and 1911 newspaper pages (public domain; check each file).
**notification:** someone walked out of the louvre with the mona lisa under a smock.
**takeaway:** fame is sometimes an accident that gets reported.

## 5. how india learned to drink chai
**fields:** food + history + advertising
**scene:** a free cup of tea pressed into your hand at howrah station in the early 1900s.
**agreed:** the indian tea association (1881); the tea cess bill of 1903 funded marketing; free prepared tea and paper packets sold for a pice at howrah; demonstrations in towns and factories; progress was slow, and by 1930 most of the crop was still exported; gandhi and other nationalists criticised tea as an imperial drink.
**contested:** counts of gramophones and records, the 1905 report numbers and the committee's later renaming dates; leave numbers out until archive-checked. "chai wallahs invented masala chai" claims are folklore: say "some say".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Tea_Association | https://deccanherald.com/features/tea-with-a-dash-of-unity-1170422.html
**visual:** vintage tea advertisements (check licence and age).
**notification:** the british paid people to hand out free chai.
**takeaway:** a "tradition" can have a marketing department.

## 6. how "ok" got started
**fields:** language + history
**scene:** boston, march 1839. a newspaper editor jokes that "o.k." stands for "oll korrect".
**agreed:** allen walker read traced the earliest known print use to the *boston morning post*, 23 march 1839, in a fad for comic abbreviations; in 1840 martin van buren's supporters ("old kinderhook") spread it with "ok clubs".
**contested:** an italian source claims a slightly earlier 1838 abbreviation; treat 1839 as the earliest confirmed standalone "ok".
**sources:** https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/how-one-man-discovered-the-obscure-origins-of-the-word-ok-180953258/ | https://wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/ok-okay
**visual:** the 1839 newspaper clipping (public domain; confirm).
**notification:** the most used word on earth started as a joke about bad spelling.
**takeaway:** the most ordinary word was once someone's inside joke.

## 7. the record we sent to the stars
**fields:** music + science + culture
**scene:** 1977. two copper records are bolted to two spacecraft, with music chosen for an audience that may never exist.
**agreed:** the voyager golden record; project led by carl sagan, ann druyan creative director; chuck berry's "johnny b. goode" was included and some argued against it (sagan reportedly said "there are a lot of adolescents on the planet"); an indian raga recorded by kesarbai kerkar represents india; the selection team included alan lomax and timothy ferris; the record also holds a recording of ann druyan's brainwaves.
**contested:** length of the brainwave recording and exact track titles: take from nasa's official list before writing; avoid "90 minutes".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record | https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/sounds/
**visual:** nasa images of the record cover and its diagram (nasa media is generally public domain; confirm each item).
**notification:** humanity's mixtape is leaving the solar system, and it includes a raga.
**takeaway:** what you choose to send says what you think matters.

## 8. the ending sholay wasn't allowed
**fields:** cinema + law + history
**scene:** 1975 india. the hero kills the villain, and the censor board says no.
**agreed:** the original ending had thakur killing gabbar with his spiked shoes; censors objected to a former police officer taking the law into his own hands; the ending was reshot with police arriving to arrest gabbar; sippy said he was unhappy but did it; sanjeev kumar was called back from a festival in russia to shoot it; a restored cut with about six minutes of extra footage and the original ending was shown in 2025.
**contested:** exact wording of the objections and how much the emergency shaped it; present as "the censors objected" and attribute to sippy.
**sources:** https://gulfnews.com/entertainment/bollywood/sholay-the-final-cut-returns-with-original-ending-and-restored-4k-magic-1.500373125 | https://www.etvbharat.com/en/entertainment/ramesh-sippy-interview-on-sholay-rerelease-after-50-years-enn25121201115
**visual:** film stills are copyrighted: use our own illustration of the two endings.
**notification:** for fifty years, you saw the wrong ending of sholay.
**takeaway:** the version you know can be the censored one.

## 9. the oldest zero
**fields:** history + mathematics + india
**scene:** a birch-bark manuscript found in a village near peshawar in 1881, with a row of dots.
**agreed:** the bakhshali manuscript; oxford's 2017 radiocarbon dating put some folios at about 300 ce, 700 ce and 900 ce; the dots are an early placeholder zero; the gwalior temple zero is dated 9th century.
**contested:** strongly. historians argued the text was written in one go on leaves of different ages and so dates to the youngest, about the 8th century; the dots work as a placeholder rather than a number. write "some say the oldest zero, others say not so fast".
**sources:** https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/dating-ancient-indian-text-gives-new-timeline-history-zero-180964896/ | https://www.medievalists.net/2017/11/historians-challenge-oxfords-claim-worlds-oldest-zero/
**visual:** bodleian images (check licence) or our own drawing of dots in a row.
**notification:** the oldest zero is a row of dots on a piece of bark. maybe.
**takeaway:** nothing is a hard thing to write down.

## 10. this is not a pipe
**fields:** art + philosophy + language
**scene:** a perfectly painted pipe, and under it, "this is not a pipe".
**agreed:** rené magritte's *the treachery of images* (1929, oil on canvas, lacma); magritte is quoted saying that writing "this is a pipe" would have been a lie; lacma reads it as about the gap between word, image and object.
**contested:** foucault's essay on the painting needs its own source before it is mentioned.
**sources:** https://collections.lacma.org/node/239578 | https://smarthistory.org/rene-magritte-the-treachery-of-images-ceci-nest-pas-une-pipe
**visual:** the painting is still in copyright (magritte died 1967): do not reproduce. draw our own pipe and caption.
**notification:** the most famous painting about a pipe says it isn't one.
**takeaway:** a picture of a thing is not the thing.

## 11. the 176 pictures that became emoji
**fields:** design + language + technology
**scene:** 1999 japan. a team has a screen the size of a thumbnail and no room for words.
**agreed:** shigetaka kurita, on the i-mode team at ntt docomo, led the design of 176 pictograms on a 12 by 12 pixel grid; they drew on manga, zapf dingbats and emoticons; moma added the originals in 2016 (a gift from ntt docomo).
**contested:** "kurita invented emoji" is a simplification; he was part of a team, and similar pictograms existed on a docomo pager.
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigetaka_Kurita | https://www.designboom.com/design/moma-acquires-original-emoji-set-collection-10-27-2016/
**visual:** moma's images are copyrighted: redraw a few as our own 12 by 12 pixel art.
**notification:** emoji started as 176 tiny pictures to save space.
**takeaway:** constraints invent new languages.

## 12. the stone that cracked egyptian
**fields:** language + history
**scene:** 1799, a french soldier digs a dark stone out of a wall near rashid.
**agreed:** a decree of ptolemy v from 196 bc, written three times (hieroglyphic, demotic, greek); found in 1799 by french soldiers; passed to the british around 1801 to 1802; thomas young made early progress (the cartouche, reading direction); champollion announced the decipherment in 1822 and showed hieroglyphs mixed alphabetic, syllabic and picture signs.
**contested:** the exact year the stone changed hands (1801 or 1802); how much credit champollion gave young (a famous rivalry).
**sources:** https://www.britannica.com/print/article/509988 | https://history.rcp.ac.uk/blog/thomas-young-and-decipherment-hieroglyphics
**visual:** a public-domain photo of the stone from wikimedia commons (confirm file).
**notification:** a soldier found a stone that solved a 1,400-year-old puzzle.
**takeaway:** you crack a code by comparing it with one you can read.

## 13. the forgetting curve
**fields:** psychology + learning
**scene:** 1880s germany. a man memorises hundreds of nonsense syllables to see how fast they vanish.
**agreed:** hermann ebbinghaus's work (usually dated 1885) measured how much easier relearning was after a gap; a 2015 plos one replication by murre and dros, with one person over about 70 hours, found similar results and a small bump after 24 hours.
**contested:** the numbers differ between secondary sources: read the plos paper before quoting any percentage. note ebbinghaus measured "savings", not raw recall.
**sources:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0120644.html
**visual:** our own chart (no licence issue).
**notification:** you forget most of what you read within days. here is the fix.
**takeaway:** say it out loud and the curve flattens (ties directly to the app's idea).

## 14. the man who fainted at the frescoes (stendhal syndrome)
**fields:** literature + art + psychology
**scene:** florence, santa croce, 1817. a writer walks out dizzy, heart racing.
**agreed:** stendhal's 1817 travel memoir describes palpitations and fear of falling after santa croce; graziella magherini named "stendhal syndrome" in 1979 and later published a book (1989) on hospital cases in florence.
**contested:** strongly. it is not in the dsm-5, and many doctors doubt it exists as a distinct condition. the "106 tourists" figure traces to magherini's own book and could not be checked. frame it as a story people tell, "some say".
**sources:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9470992/ | https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/stendhal-syndrome-can-the-beauty-of-art-make-us-ill
**visual:** public-domain image of santa croce or a frescoed interior (check file).
**notification:** can art make you ill? florence's doctors argue.
**takeaway:** beauty can overwhelm you, or at least a good story can.

## 15. the dabbawalas and a statistic nobody checked
**fields:** culture + logistics
**scene:** 12 noon, mumbai. a lunchbox travels across the city without a computer.
**agreed:** service dates to 1890 (mahadeo havaji bachche); a charitable trust in 1956 and a commercial association in 1968.
**contested:** the famous "one error in 16 million" and "six sigma" claims. the forbes writer says the figure was an extrapolation from a spokesperson's rough remark ("almost never, maybe once every two months") and that forbes never certified six sigma. write the story as "a famous number nobody checked".
**sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dabbawala
**visual:** wikimedia commons photos (check each licence).
**notification:** the most famous statistic about mumbai's lunchboxes may be made up.
**takeaway:** a number repeated enough starts to feel true.

## 16. the wilhelm scream
**fields:** cinema + sound + history
**scene:** a soldier is dragged into a swamp by an alligator in 1951, and a sound is reborn.
**agreed:** recorded for *distant drums* (1951); named after pvt. wilhelm in *the charge at feather river* (1953); ben burtt found it while working on *star wars* (1977) and has used it in the star wars and indiana jones films; cited in 400+ films.
**contested:** whose voice it is (sheb wooley is the usual guess, unconfirmed); the exact count of films.

## 17. the film paid for with pawned jewellery
**fields:** cinema + history
**scene:** calcutta, early 1950s. a graphic designer sells his records and his wife pawns her jewellery to finish a film.
**agreed:** satyajit ray sold books and records; bijoya ray pawned her jewellery; funds ran out; the west bengal government (b. c. roy) later helped; *pather panchali* released in 1955.

## 18. panini's grammar and the rule that was misread
**fields:** language + computing + india
**scene:** about 2,400 years ago, one person writes the rules of a language as a system.
**agreed:** panini's *ashtadhyayi*; its rules sometimes conflict; the traditional reading of its tie-break ("later rule wins") often gave wrong forms; rishi rajpopat's 2022 cambridge thesis argued that for rules applying to the left and right of a word the right-side rule should win, and reported correct forms with almost no exceptions; he suggested this could help teach sanskrit grammar to computers.
**contested:** press coverage called it "solved" and "2,500 years"; treat as one scholar's proposal under discussion. no source here confirms the comparison with backus-naur form: leave that out until checked.
**sources:** https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=57378 | https://www.cam.ac.uk/system/files/rajpopat_phd_thesis_15_dec_2022.pdf
**visual:** a manuscript page (check licence) or our own diagram.
**notification:** a 2,400-year-old rulebook for sanskrit was finally read the right way round. maybe.
**takeaway:** an old text can still hold a surprise.
